% Diagram 78 of the 1500-1599 book: Mohamed - Alagouri
@ 8/8/8/4k1pN/8/1K2b3/8/8 b - - 1 81 | Kf5?
This is quite a difficult position. Unlike rook endings, there is no clear theory to rely on.
Both sides should know the typical tactics and use the \term{process-of-elimination} to avoid
mistakes.\index{Practical play!Process of elimination}

A few guidelines help. The defending king is far away, so the defender must not let his knight
lose its mobility.\index{Bishop against knight!Mobility of the knight} The defender can also offer to give up the knight
for the pawn: this forces the pawn forward, and then the knight can fork.\index{Knight!Fork}

81...\B f4! restricts the knight. 82.\K c3 \K f5 83.\N g7+ \K e4 84.\N h5 \B e5+, and the knight
cannot move at all. This is called \term{domination}.\index{Bishop against knight!Domination} Black brings his king to the knight
and wins.

@ 8/8/8/6p1/5k2/1K2b1N1/8/8 w - - 4 83 | Nf1?
83.\N h5+! draws: 83...\K g4 84.\N g7 \K g3 85.\N h5+ \K h4 (85...\K g4 86.\N g7 (86.\N f6+?
\K f5 87.\N d5 \B d4 loses the knight) 86...\K f3 87.\K c4 g4 88.\N h5 \B f2 89.\K d5 \B h4)
86.\N f6 \K g3 (86...\B d4 87.\N e4 g4 88.\K c2 \B a7 89.\K d3).

@ 8/8/8/6p1/5k2/1K2b3/8/5N2 b - - 5 83 | Kf3?
83...\K e4! wins.

@ 8/8/8/6p1/8/1K2bk2/8/5N2 w - - 6 84 | Kc3?
84.\N h2+! draws: 84...\K g3 85.\N f1+ \K f2 86.\N h2 \K g2 87.\N g4 \K f3 88.\N h6 \B d4
89.\K c2 \B g7 90.\N f5 \B f6 91.\N h6 \B e7 92.\N f7 g4 93.\N e5+. The defender offers the
knight for the pawn, forces the pawn forward, and then forks.\index{Knight!Fork}

@ 8/8/8/6p1/8/2K1bk2/8/5N2 b - - 7 84 | Bf4!
\term{domination} again.\index{Bishop against knight!Domination}

% Diagram 82 of the 1500-1599 book: Japic - Muzzolini
@ 8/8/7p/5pkn/3K4/5B2/8/8 w - - 0 55 | Bxh5?
This is hopeless: against these two separated pawns the white king alone has no chance. The
correct defence is like a study.\index{Study-like positions}

55.\K e3! White must defend with both king and bishop. The first defence is to stop f3: the bishop
shuffles between d1 and e2, and the king stays on g2 to stop h3. 55...\N f6 56.\B d1 \N g4+
57.\K f3 h5 58.\B e2 \N e5+ 59.\K g3 f4+ 60.\K g2 \N c6 61.\B d1 \N d4, and Black takes the e2-square
away from the bishop.

62.\K f2. The second defence: \B e2 is no longer possible, so White shuffles his king to stop f3.
62...h4. Black tries to make progress with his h-pawn. 63.\K g2 \K f5 64.\K f2 h3 65.\B a4!.
Passive defence is no longer possible, but White still has a resource. 65...\K g4 66.\B d7+ \K h4
67.\B c8 f3 68.\B b7 h2. Black has improved his position as far as it goes, and White must give
up his bishop for the pawn: 69.\B xf3 \N xf3 70.\K g2 \K g4 71.\K h1.

The final position is a draw.\index{Theoretical draws!Knight and h-pawn against king} Black can
protect his h-pawn only with the knight, because with the king he would stalemate White. White
simply shuffles his king between g2 and h1.

% Diagram 83 of the 1500-1599 book: Solovyev - Shilnikov
@ 3B4/1k4p1/1p1n4/8/5K2/8/8/8 b - - 3 57 | b5?
57...\K c6! is the right move. The idea is simple: if Black escorts his pawn with his king, White
must give up his bishop to stop it, and the remaining ending is hopeless. The text move allows
White to set up a fortress.\index{Fortress}

@ 3B4/1k4p1/3n4/1p6/5K2/8/8/8 w - - 0 58 | Ba5?
58.\B e7! \N e8 59.\B b4 \K c6 60.\K e5 sets up the fortress. Note how it works: the bishop controls
the dark squares, and the king the light squares.\index{Fortress} The black king cannot go forward
to support his b-pawn, and the knight alone cannot protect the g-pawn. In the next few moves White
takes the g-pawn, and the remaining ending is a draw.

60...\N f6 61.\K e6 \N d7 62.\K f5 \N f6 (62...\K d5 63.\K g6) 63.\B f8 \K d5 (63...\N e8
64.\K e5 \K b6 (64...g6 65.\B a3) 65.\B b4 \N f6 66.\K f5 \N e8 67.\K e6 \K c6 68.\B a3 \N c7+)
64.\B xg7 b4 (64...\N d7 65.\K f4 \K c4 66.\K e4 b4 67.\K e3).

This misses the point: the pawn push does not help the defence. At best Black has to give up his

passed pawn and two connected pawns. Note that the h-pawn is the most dangerous one for the knight:
on the edge of the board, the knight has less room to manoeuvre.\index{Knight against pawns!Rook's pawn}

shows: 59...\B c7 60.\N a6 \B d8 61.c6 h4 62.c7 \B xc7 63.\N xc7 g3 64.\K f1, and the king arrives
in time. White has no problem defending.

59...h4 60.\N d3 g3 61.\N f2+!. If White takes the pawn, the bishop is of the wrong colour: it
cannot support the promotion, and bishop and h-pawn against king is another theoretical draw.\index{Theoretical draws!Wrong bishop and rook's pawn against king}
The defending king just stays in the corner, as in the other endings against the h-pawn.

61...\K f4 62.\N h3+. White sets up a \term{barrier}:\index{Barrier} if the black king comes

\B d4 66.c6 \B b6 67.\N d3 (67.\N h1 \K g4) 67...\K g4 68.\N e5+ \K f4 69.\N d3+ \K g4 (69...\K e4
70.\N e1) 70.\N e5+ \K f5 71.\N f3 \K g4 72.\N e5+ \K f4 73.\N d3+ \K e4 74.\N e1 \B d8 75.\K h3
\K e3 76.\N g2+ \K f2 77.\N xh4, and White draws.

This loses the knight: the black pawn queens, and the win is gone. White should have used his
knight as a decoy to lure the black king away. Catching the knight costs Black tempi, and that
gives White enough time to queen and win.

guard one diagonal: the \term{one-diagonal}.\index{Bishop!One-diagonal principle} Here the bishop is torn apart: it cannot
stop the a-pawn and the h-pawn at the same time. 50.\N f2+! \K f4 51.a5 \K g3 52.\N h1+ \K g2 53.a6
\K xh1 54.a7 f2 55.a8=\Q+.

queen against bishop's pawn is usually a draw (\xref{Section}{queen-against-pawn}). Now White cannot stop Black from promoting, and
Black's remaining bishop will defend the pawn easily.\index{Theoretical draws!Queen against bishop's pawn}

% Diagram 100 of the 1500-1599 book: Ozaltun - Vatansever
@ 8/8/6p1/1B2n1p1/5p2/4k1PP/6K1/8 w - - 0 56 | g4?
The correct defence is quite instructive. 56.gxf4! In a worse position, exchanging pawns is a good
idea.\index{Exchanging or removing the pawns} 56...gxf4 57.h4!. Remember this resource. The h3-pawn is weak, so why not move it to a better
square? As we will see, this also frees h3 for the bishop, which then defends against Black's
\term{interference}.\index{Bishop against knight!Interference}\index{Strategy!Prophylaxis}
Black's idea is to win the weak h3-pawn little by little: 57.\B a6? \N f3 58.\K f1 \N h4 59.\B e2
f3 60.\B a6 f2 61.\B b5 \K f3 62.\B e2+ \K g3 63.\B d3 \N f3 64.h4 \N xh4.

63.\K g3 \N b3 64.\K g4 \N c5 65.\B f1) 58.h5!. This is the whole idea: White gives up the pawn for
a while. 58...gxh5 59.\B e8 h4 60.\K h3 \N d4 61.\K xh4, and the ending is a draw: 61...f3 62.\B d7
f2 63.\B h3.

The defence is difficult, but you can find it step by step: first exchange pawns, then remove your
own weakness. Little by little, White reaches the draw.
