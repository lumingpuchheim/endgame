% Diagram 78 of the 1500-1599 book: Mohamed - Alagouri
@ 8/8/8/4k1pN/8/1K2b3/8/8 b - - 1 81 | Kf5?
This is quite a difficult position. Unlike rook endings, there is no clear theory to rely on.
Both sides should know the typical tactics and use the \term{process-of-elimination} to avoid
mistakes.\index{Practical play!Process of elimination}

A few guidelines help. The defending king is far away, so the defender must not let his knight
lose its mobility.\index{Bishop against knight!Mobility of the knight} The defender can also offer to give up the knight
for the pawn: this forces the pawn forward, and then the knight can fork.\index{Knight fork}

81...\B f4! restricts the knight. 82.\K c3 \K f5 83.\N g7+ \K e4 84.\N h5 \B e5+, and the knight
cannot move at all. This is called \vocab[strategy]{domination}{domination}{A piece is attacked
and has no safe square to go to.}.\index{Bishop against knight!Domination} Black brings his king to the knight
and wins.

@ 8/8/8/6p1/5k2/1K2b1N1/8/8 w - - 4 83 | Nf1?
83.\N h5+! draws: 83...\K g4 84.\N g7 \K g3 85.\N h5+ \K h4 (85...\K g4 86.\N g7 (86.\N f6+?
\K f5 87.\N d5 \B d4 loses the knight) 86...\K f3 87.\K c4 g4 88.\N h5 \B f2 89.\K d5 \B h4)
86.\N f6 \K g3 (86...\B d4 87.\N e4 g4 88.\K c2 \B a7 89.\K d3).

@ 8/8/8/6p1/5k2/1K2b3/8/5N2 b - - 5 83 | Kf3?
83...\K e4! wins.

@ 8/8/8/6p1/8/1K2bk2/8/5N2 w - - 6 84 | Kc3?
84.\N h2+! draws: 84...\K g3 85.\N f1+ \K f2 86.\N h2 \K g2 87.\N g4 \K f3 88.\N h6 \B d4
89.\K c2 \B g7 90.\N f5 \B f6 91.\N h6 \B e7 92.\N f7 g4 93.\N e5+. The defender offers the
knight for the pawn, forces the pawn forward, and then forks.\index{Knight fork}

@ 8/8/8/6p1/8/2K1bk2/8/5N2 b - - 7 84 | Bf4!
\term{domination} again.\index{Bishop against knight!Domination}

% Diagram 79 of the 1500-1599 book
@ 8/8/8/5B2/8/1nk5/1p3K2/8 w - - 11 66 | Bb1?
A typical way for knight and pawn to beat the bishop is \vocab[strategy]{interference}{interference}{A
piece is put on a line to cut it, so that an enemy piece can no longer guard a square through
it.}.\index{Bishop against knight!Interference} Black threatens to bring his king to c1 and cut the bishop off
from b1 with \N c2. White must stop this, and the defence is simple: 66.\K e1! \N d4 67.\B b1
\N c2+ 68.\K d1 \N e3+ 69.\K e1 \N c2+ 70.\K d1, and Black makes no progress.

% Diagram 80 of the 1500-1599 book
@ 8/8/4N1p1/4P3/K3k3/4b3/8/8 w - - 1 79 | Ng7?
White has a clear-cut draw:\index{Practical play!Clear-cut draw} attack the pawn and fork.\index{Knight fork}
79.\N f8! g5 80.\N h7 g4 81.\N f6+ \K xe5 82.\N xg4.

% Diagram 81 of the 1500-1599 book
@ 8/2P5/2K1b3/3N3p/7k/8/8/8 w - - 0 78 | Nf6?
White has the right idea, \term{interference},\index{Bishop against knight!Interference} but the wrong move.
78.\N b6! and then \N d7 wins. Black cannot answer with \B c8, because the knight covers that
square.

% Diagram 82 of the 1500-1599 book: Japic - Muzzolini
@ 8/8/7p/5pkn/3K4/5B2/8/8 w - - 0 55 | Bxh5?
This is hopeless: against these two separated pawns the white king alone has no chance. The
correct defence is like a study.\index{Study-like positions}

55.\K e3! White must defend with both king and bishop. The first defence is to stop f3: the bishop
shuffles between d1 and e2, and the king stays on g2 to stop h3. 55...\N f6 56.\B d1 \N g4+
57.\K f3 h5 58.\B e2 \N e5+ 59.\K g3 f4+ 60.\K g2 \N c6 61.\B d1 \N d4, and Black takes the e2-square
away from the bishop.

62.\K f2. The second defence: \B e2 is no longer possible, so White shuffles his king to stop f3.
62...h4. Black tries to make progress with his h-pawn. 63.\K g2 \K f5 64.\K f2 h3 65.\B a4!.
Passive defence is no longer possible, but White still has a resource. 65...\K g4 66.\B d7+ \K h4
67.\B c8 f3 68.\B b7 h2. Black has improved his position as far as it goes, and White must give
up his bishop for the pawn: 69.\B xf3 \N xf3 70.\K g2 \K g4 71.\K h1.

The final position is a draw.\index{Theoretical draws!Knight and h-pawn against king} Black can
protect his h-pawn only with the knight, because with the king he would stalemate White. White
simply shuffles his king between g2 and h1.

% Diagram 83 of the 1500-1599 book: Solovyev - Shilnikov
@ 3B4/1k4p1/1p1n4/8/5K2/8/8/8 b - - 3 57 | b5?
57...\K c6! is the right move. The idea is simple: if Black escorts his pawn with his king, White
must give up his bishop to stop it, and the remaining ending is hopeless. The text move allows
White to set up a fortress.\index{Fortress}

@ 3B4/1k4p1/3n4/1p6/5K2/8/8/8 w - - 0 58 | Ba5?
58.\B e7! \N e8 59.\B b4 \K c6 60.\K e5 sets up the fortress. Note how it works: the bishop controls
the dark squares, and the king the light squares.\index{Fortress} The black king cannot go forward
to support his b-pawn, and the knight alone cannot protect the g-pawn. In the next few moves White
takes the g-pawn, and the remaining ending is a draw.

60...\N f6 61.\K e6 \N d7 62.\K f5 \N f6 (62...\K d5 63.\K g6) 63.\B f8 \K d5 (63...\N e8
64.\K e5 \K b6 (64...g6 65.\B a3) 65.\B b4 \N f6 66.\K f5 \N e8 67.\K e6 \K c6 68.\B a3 \N c7+)
64.\B xg7 b4 (64...\N d7 65.\K f4 \K c4 66.\K e4 b4 67.\K e3).

% Diagram 84 of the 1500-1599 book: Papageorgiou - Tsolaki
@ 8/7p/6p1/2NK1k2/2P4b/8/8/8 b - - 0 53 | Be7?!
According to the endgame tablebase, this move does not throw away the win. 53...h5 is easier
(54.\K d4? \B f2+).

@ 8/4b2p/6p1/2NK1k2/2P5/8/8/8 w - - 1 54 | Nd3
54.\K d4 is necessary: the king must go to the king side to defend. According to the tablebase,
this move loses too.

@ 8/4b2p/6p1/3K1k2/2P5/3N4/8/8 b - - 2 54 | h5?
54...\B f6! 55.c5 h5 56.c6 \B d8 57.\K d6 h4 wins.

@ 8/4b3/6p1/3K1k1p/2P5/3N4/8/8 w - - 0 55 | c5?
This misses the point: the pawn push does not help the defence. At best Black has to give up his
bishop for the pawn, but White's king is far away, and his knight alone cannot defend against a
passed pawn and two connected pawns. Note that the h-pawn is the most dangerous one for the knight:
on the edge of the board, the knight has less room to manoeuvre.\index{Knight against pawns!Rook's pawn}

55.\K d4! is necessary: the king helps the knight to defend. 55...g5 56.\K e3 \B d8 57.\K e2
\K e4 58.c5 g4 59.\N b4!. The knight is ready to support the pawn if necessary, as the next line
shows: 59...\B c7 60.\N a6 \B d8 61.c6 h4 62.c7 \B xc7 63.\N xc7 g3 64.\K f1, and the king arrives
in time. White has no problem defending.

59...h4 60.\N d3 g3 61.\N f2+!. If White takes the pawn, the bishop is of the wrong colour: it
cannot support the promotion, and bishop and h-pawn against king is another theoretical draw.\index{Theoretical draws!Wrong bishop and h-pawn against king}
The defending king just stays in the corner, as in the other endings against the h-pawn.

61...\K f4 62.\N h3+. White sets up a \emph{barrier}:\index{Knight against pawns!Barrier} if the black king comes
closer, the knight drives it away with a check. 62...\K g4 63.\N f2+ \K f5 64.\K f3 \B f6 65.\K g2
\B d4 66.c6 \B b6 67.\N d3 (67.\N h1 \K g4) 67...\K g4 68.\N e5+ \K f4 69.\N d3+ \K g4 (69...\K e4
70.\N e1) 70.\N e5+ \K f5 71.\N f3 \K g4 72.\N e5+ \K f4 73.\N d3+ \K e4 74.\N e1 \B d8 75.\K h3
\K e3 76.\N g2+ \K f2 77.\N xh4, and White draws.

% Diagram 85 of the 1500-1599 book: Pasqualotto - Marge
@ 5n2/8/3k1p2/PK6/6P1/8/4B3/8 b - - 0 62 | Ne6?
The a-pawn is the real danger, and it must be stopped: 62...\K c7! 63.a6 \K b8 64.\K b6 (64.\K c6
\K a7 65.\K d5 \N g6 66.\K e4 \N e7. Black sets up a barrier:\index{Knight against pawns!Barrier}
the white king cannot pass through d5, e5 or f5, and going via c5 allows Black to give up the knight
for both pawns. 67.\K d4 \N c6+ 68.\K c5 \N e5 69.\K d5 \N xg4 70.\B xg4 \K xa6) 64...\N d7+
65.\K c6 \N e5+ 66.\K d6 \K a7 67.\K c5 \N xg4 68.\B xg4 \K xa6. Again a typical defence: the
knight is given up for the pawns.\index{Giving up a piece for the pawns}

% Diagram 87 of the 1500-1599 book: Sari - Eremkere
@ 8/8/7P/1Kp1n1p1/3k4/5B2/8/8 w - - 1 64 | h7?
\emph{Do not rush!}\index{Strategy!Do not rush} The h-pawn is White's weapon, and Black must play
\N g6 or \N f7 to stop it. 64.\B h5! stops both, and then h7 wins the game.

@ 8/7P/6n1/1Kp3p1/3k4/5B2/8/8 w - - 1 65 | Bd1?
Now White loses, because Black starts to push his pawns. 65.\B h5 \N h8 66.\B e2 draws: the bishop
keeps an eye on both c4 and g4. 66...\K d5 67.\B c4+ \K d4 68.\B e2.

% Diagram 88 of the 1500-1599 book: Rojas - Rahaman
@ 8/3B4/3n1k2/2P4p/p4K2/8/P7/8 b - - 0 48 | Nb7?
This gives White two free tempi with the pawn, and the position becomes hopeless. Black has
several ways to draw, and all of them aim at removing the weak c-pawn.\index{Eliminate the pawn}
48...\K e7 (48...\N c4 49.\B xa4 \K e7 50.\B b5 \N a3 51.\B d3 \K d7) 49.\B xa4 \N b7 50.c6 \N c5
51.\B d1 \K d6 52.\B f3 \N e6+ 53.\K g3 \N d8, and the c-pawn falls.

% Petrov - Orzechowski
@ 8/6pp/8/4N3/3kb3/1K4P1/7P/8 w - - 1 55 | Nf7?
Here the move can be found with the \term{process-of-elimination}:\index{Practical play!Process of elimination}
55.\N f7 \B d5+, 55.\N g4 \B f3 56.\N f2 g5 57.h4 g4 58.\K b4 \K e3, and 55.\N c4 \B d5 all lose
the knight.

55.\N d7! is the only move left: 55...h5 56.\K b2 \K e3 57.\K c3 \K f2 58.\K d4 \B f3 59.\K e5 \B g4
60.\N c5 \K g2 61.\K f4 \K xh2 62.\N e4 g6 63.\N d2 \K g2 64.\N e4, and White has set up a
fortress.\index{Fortress} If Black tries to put him in zugzwang with 64...\B c8 65.\K g5 \B f5,
White draws by removing the pawns:\index{Eliminate the pawn} 66.\N f6 \K xg3 67.\N xh5+ gxh5
68.\K xh5.

% Shimamura Silva - Benevenga
@ 8/8/2p4p/2n1p1k1/8/4K3/7P/3B4 b - - 3 50 | Kf5?
The c6-pawn behind the knight on c5 is a weakness. Removing his own weakness gives Black the win:\index{Strategy!Remove your own weakness}
50...\N e6! 51.\K e4 \K f6 52.\B g4 \N d4 53.\B d7 c5 54.\K d5 \N f3 55.\K xc5 \N xh2.

@ 8/8/2p4p/2n1pk2/8/4K3/7P/3B4 w - - 4 51 | h3?
White misses his chance. 51.\B f3! e4 (otherwise the c6-pawn is lost) 52.\B g2, and White just
waits. Black has to give up a pawn to make progress: 52...\K e6 53.\K d4 \K d6 54.\B h1.

Exchanging the pieces with 53.\B xe4? \N xe4 54.\K xe4 loses: 54...h5 55.h4 c5 56.\K d3 \K f5
57.\K c4 \K g4 58.\K xc5 \K xh4. The pawn ending can be judged at a glance with Bähr's rule
(\xref{Section}{bahr-rule}).\index{King and pawn endings!Bähr's rule} Here the colours and the wings are reversed: Black is the stronger side, and the key diagonal is
a6--f1. The rook's pawns on h4 and h5 stand as in the normal position, but the c5-pawn is one
square behind the diagonal. Black has one extra tempo, and wins.

% Diagram 93 of the 1500-1599 book: Daudaravicius - Boyajyan
@ 8/K2N3b/8/2Pk3P/5p2/8/P7/8 w - - 0 44 | Nf6+?
The c5-pawn is White's weakness: deal with it first.\index{Strategy!Remove your own weakness}
44.\K b6! \K d4 45.\N f6 \B f5 46.h6 f3 wins. After the text move, 44...\K xc5? loses to 45.\N xh7
f3 46.\N g5 f2 47.\N e4+, a knight fork again,\index{Knight fork} but Black is alert enough not to
fall for it.

@ 8/8/1K5P/5b2/P3k1N1/5p2/8/8 w - - 1 50 | a5?
This loses the knight: the black pawn queens, and the win is gone. White should have used his
knight as a decoy to lure the black king away. Catching the knight costs Black tempi, and that
gives White enough time to queen and win.

When a bishop defends against passed pawns, it is good for the defender if the bishop only has to
guard one diagonal.\index{One-diagonal principle (bishop)} Here the bishop is torn apart: it cannot
stop the a-pawn and the h-pawn at the same time. 50.\N f2+! \K f4 51.a5 \K g3 52.\N h1+ \K g2 53.a6
\K xh1 54.a7 f2 55.a8=\Q+.
