King and pawn endings are usually decided by \term{zugzwang}.\index{Zugzwang}
65.\K c2? lets Black choose between 65...\K b4 and the \term{opposition} with 65...\K c4. Which of the two is right does not matter to White. The calculation is not difficult, so Black will probably find the right move, and as long as one of the two wins, White loses.

This is worth remembering beyond pawn endings: give your opponent no \term{easy-choice}.\index{Practical play!No easy choice} To find 65.\K b3 you do not need to work out whether 65...\K b4 or 65...\K c4 wins. @ 8/p7/1k6/8/PPK5/8/8/8 b - - 0 51 | a5?
\term{do-not-weaken}.\index{Strategy!Do not weaken yourself} 51...a5? gives White a passed pawn, and after 51...a6 the white
king goes to b6 and the pawn on a6 falls.

Black can simply leave the pawn on a7 and move his king between a8 and b8.\index{King and pawn endings!A- and b-pawns against an a-pawn}
White's try is to set up the pawns on a5 and b6, with the king on a6. Black's king shuffles
between a8 and b8. If the black king is on b8 and it is Black's move, he draws.
\diagram[king-a6-black-b8]{1k6/p7/KP6/P7/8/8/8/8 b - - 0 1}
1...axb6 2.axb6 \K a8 3.b7+ \K b8 4.\K b6, stalemate.\index{King and pawn endings!Stalemate}

If the black king is on a8 and it is Black's move, he loses.
\diagram[king-a6-black-a8]{k7/p7/KP6/P7/8/8/8/8 b - - 0 1}
1...\K b8 2.b7 \K c7 3.\K xa7, and the pawn queens.

So when White's king stands on b5 and Black's king has to retreat from b7, Black must choose the
right square, a8 or b8. If one of White's pawns is still on its original square after Black has
committed himself, White wins, because he can choose to push that pawn one square or two.

How does Black choose? He must arrange that, when White's pawns reach a5 and b6 with the king on
a6, his king stands on b8 and it is his move (\xref{Diagram}{king-a6-black-b8}), not on a8
(\xref{Diagram}{king-a6-black-a8}). Every pawn move by White changes the count, so Black decides only
after White has committed his pawn.
\diagram[king-b5-a2-pawn]{8/pk6/8/1K6/1P6/8/P7/8 w - - 0 1}
With White to move, Black draws. After 1.a3, the a-pawn still needs two moves to reach a5, so the
king goes to b8: 1.a3 \K b8! 2.\K a6 \K a8 3.a4 \K b8 4.a5 \K a8 5.b5 \K b8 6.b6, and we have
\xref{Diagram}{king-a6-black-b8}: Black to move draws. After 1.a4, one pawn move fewer is left, so the
king goes to a8: 1.a4 \K a8! 2.\K a6 \K b8 3.a5 \K a8 4.b5 \K b8 5.b6, again
\xref{Diagram}{king-a6-black-b8}.

With Black to move, the same position is lost, because now Black has to commit first. White answers
with the right pawn move: one square or two, whichever brings Black into
\xref{Diagram}{king-a6-black-a8}. After 1...\K b8 2.\K a6 \K a8 White plays 3.a3!, the single step:
3...\K b8 4.a4 \K a8 5.a5 \K b8 6.b5 \K a8 7.b6. After 1...\K a8 2.\K a6 \K b8 White plays 3.a4!, the
double step: 3...\K a8 4.a5 \K b8 5.b5 \K a8 6.b6. Either way, the black king is on a8 with Black to
move, and White wins. The pawn on its original square gives White the choice, and that choice
decides the game.

Back to the diagram: both of White's pawns have already left their original squares, so Black can
hold. He must only choose between a8 and b8 when he has to; otherwise his king tries to push the
white king away.

% Diagram 43 of the 2600+ book: Aravindh - Safarli
@ 8/8/2P5/p2k2p1/P2p4/3K2P1/8/8 b - - 0 41 | Kxc6?
On the g-file both sides have a \term{reserve-tempo}:\index{King and pawn endings!Reserve tempo} when one side's king is opposed, he can play g4
(or ...g4) to take the \term{opposition}\index{King and pawn endings!Opposition} against the enemy
king. Black is not obliged to take the pawn at once: 41...\K d6! 42.\K xd4 \K xc6 43.\K c4 g4. Now
White must make a concession: 44.\K d4 \K d6 45.\K e4 \K c5!, \term{outflanking},\index{King and pawn endings!Outflanking}
and the pawns will be exchanged. Note that Black must not follow White's king: 45...\K e6? 46.\K f4
\K f6 47.\K xg4, and the position is lost according to Bähr's rule.\index{King and pawn endings!Bähr's rule}
