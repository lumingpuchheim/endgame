\chapter{How to Use This Book}

\section*{Why another endgame book?}
If you want to study endgames thoroughly, there is already a wonderful book for that: Mark
Dvoretsky's \emph{Endgame Manual}~\cite{Dvoretsky:2008-endgame-manual}. It covers the whole
subject, from king and pawn against king to the most delicate rook endings, and a determined
amateur can work through it. We have no intention of repeating his work.

But reading is not the same as learning. To really learn an endgame, you have to see the same
idea again and again, try it yourself, get it wrong, take a break, and come back to it a few days
later. A manual explains each idea once, clearly and completely, and then moves on. This book
tries to give you the rest: the repetition and the practice.

\section*{Study where the points are lost}
This book is pragmatic. Instead of starting with the first page of endgame theory, it asks a
simple question: in which endgames do players actually lose points in tournament games? We let
a chess engine check every endgame in thousands of real games and counted every move that turned
a win into a draw, or a draw into a loss. \xref{Part}{part:mistakes} shows what came out, and
the endgames that cost the most points come first in the rest of the book.

\section*{Made for your rating band}
A grandmaster and a club player do not go wrong in the same places. That is why each edition of
this book is built from the games of one narrow rating band, which you can see on the cover. The
positions come from players of that level, so they should be neither too hard nor too easy for
you.

\section*{Practise, practise, practise}
\xref{Part}{part:endgames} gives you plenty of positions, one endgame per chapter, from the
simplest to the more complex. In every one of them, a real player went wrong. Cover the moves
below the diagram and decide what you would play. Then read on and see what happened in the game.

Every diagram has a link, \emph{Open on Lichess}. Click it, and the position opens on Lichess,
where you can play it out against the computer as often as you like. Do get it wrong there: it
costs nothing, and it is the best way to remember. And when your head is full, stop and come back
tomorrow.

\section*{Little theory, easy to find}
We keep theory to a minimum. Where an idea is needed, it is written on a short card, one idea per
card, such as side checks in rook endings. The words that matter are collected in the glossary at
the end of the book, each with the kind of endgame it belongs to.

The comments on the games do more than give variations and explanations: they also point to the
cards. The index at the end lists, for every card, the pages that refer to it. The more pages a
card has, the more often its idea decides real games, and the more it is worth knowing.

\section*{How the book is organised}
\xref{Chapter}{ch:meet} shows which endgames you will meet most often. \xref{Chapter}{ch:go-wrong}
shows which ones go wrong most often once they are on the board. \xref{Chapter}{ch:where-to-start}
puts the two together and sets the order of study. From there on, each chapter takes one endgame
from that list. The appendix explains how the numbers were made.

Enjoy the positions, and good luck in your next tournament!
