\chapter{How to Use This Book}

\section*{Why another endgame book?}
If you want to study endgames thoroughly, there is already a wonderful book for that: Mark
Dvoretsky's \emph{Endgame Manual}~\cite{Dvoretsky:2008-endgame-manual}. It covers the whole
subject, from king and pawn against king to the most delicate rook endings, and a determined
amateur can work through it. We have no intention of repeating his work.

But reading is not the same as learning. To really learn an endgame, you have to see the same
idea again and again, try it yourself, get it wrong, take a break, and come back to it a few days
later. A manual explains each idea once, clearly and completely, and then moves on. This book
tries to give you the rest: the repetition and the practice.

\section*{Study where the points are lost}
This book is pragmatic. Instead of starting with the first page of endgame theory, it asks a
simple question: in which endgames do players actually lose points in tournament games? We let
a chess engine check every endgame in thousands of real games and counted every move that turned
a win into a draw, or a draw into a loss. \xref{Part}{part:mistakes} shows what came out, and
the endgames that cost the most points come first in the rest of the book.

\section*{Made for your rating band}
A grandmaster and a club player do not go wrong in the same places. That is why each edition of
this book is built from the games of one narrow rating band, which you can see on the cover. The
positions come from players of that level, so they should be neither too hard nor too easy for
you.

\section*{Practise, practise, practise}
\xref{Part}{part:endgames} gives you plenty of positions, one endgame per chapter, from the
simplest to the more complex. In every one of them, a real player went wrong. Cover the moves
below the diagram and decide what you would play. Then read on and see what happened in the game.

Reading is not enough, so every section ends with exercises: more positions of the same kind,
from other games, where again a player went wrong. Here the book gives neither the moves nor the
answer. You find it yourself.

Practise the right way. Before you check anything, write down what you think: the plan, the moves
you considered, and the move you would play, with the reason. Only then click \emph{Open on
Lichess}, turn on the computer, and compare. Where the computer disagrees, go back to your notes.
Was it a move you did not see, a plan you did not know, or a mistake in calculation? These are the
holes in your thinking, and they are exactly what you need to work on. Without your notes you would
only learn the right move; with them you learn why you missed it.

You can also play each position out against the computer as often as you like. Do get it wrong
there: it costs nothing, and it is the best way to remember. And when your head is full, stop and
come back tomorrow.

\section*{Little theory, easy to find}
We keep theory to a minimum. Where an idea is needed, such as side checks in rook endings, it is
explained briefly where it first comes up. The words that matter are collected in the glossary at
the end of the book, each with the kind of endgame it belongs to.

Every idea is an entry in the index at the end of the book, and the index lists every page where
the idea appears in a game. The more pages an idea has, the more often it decides real games, and
the more it is worth knowing.

\section*{How to reach a decision quickly}
Alexander Kotov taught generations of players to think in candidate moves: list the sensible
moves first, then analyse each one once, branch by branch, like a
tree~\cite{Kotov:1971-think-like-a-grandmaster}. It is good advice for a computer. A computer never
forgets a branch and never tires of seeing a position that is not on the board. A human being
does both. Holding a tree of variations in your head costs enormous mental energy, and the energy
you spend early in a game is missing in the endgame, where most points are lost. So we should not
try to think like a computer. We should think like a human being who knows where to save effort.

\emph{Plans or moves?} We think at two levels during a game, sometimes about a plan and sometimes
about a single move, and the position tells us which. Ask first: is something forcing going on?
If your king is in check, a piece is attacked, a capture must be answered or a pawn is about to
queen, there is no time for plans. Think in moves, and look for the right answer to the threat.
If nothing is forcing, and especially in an unclear ending, think in plans first: rear checks or
side checks, a fortress, eliminating the last pawn, bringing the king to the short side. There are
only a few plans in any position, and choosing between them is much easier than choosing between
moves. Once the plan is chosen, the moves often follow almost by themselves. Then ask the question
again after every move, because a quiet position can become forcing in one move, and back again.

\emph{Know the typical themes.} You can only consider a plan you know. That is what this book is
for: every idea in it has an entry in the index, and the more often it appears, the more often it
will be a candidate in your own games.

\emph{Calculate forcing lines first.} When you must calculate, begin with the moves that limit the
opponent's answers: exchanges, checks, threats. Mark Dvoretsky put it plainly: ``In a practical
game, it makes sense to extend our analysis only to the end of a forcing
variation''~\cite{Dvoretsky:2008-analytical-manual}. And if a forcing line gives you the result
you want, a clear-cut draw or a clean win, take it. Do not look for something better.

\emph{Eliminate before you go deep.} Many moves can be ruled out after a short calculation: you
check whether the opponent has a simple answer, and if he has, the move is gone, without following
it any further. That is the process of elimination. In a bad position it is your first tool:
before looking for the best move, rule out the moves that lose at once.

Charlie Munger made business decisions the same way. He did not try to be brilliant. He said it is
remarkable how much long-term advantage people like Warren Buffett and himself gained ``by trying
to be consistently not stupid''~\cite{Munger:2005-almanack}. He began with the question of what to
avoid: ``All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go
there''~\cite{Sorsaburu:avoid-ineptitude}, and he followed the mathematician Carl Jacobi's advice
to ``invert, always invert''. And he judged new situations quickly because he carried a set of
models for the typical ones. That is exactly the method of this book: know the typical endgame
ideas, rule out what loses, calculate only where it is forced, and save your strength for the
moments that really need it.

\emph{Some positions are studies.} A few positions in this book are like endgame studies. We do
not expect you to find the right move by calculation, and you should not expect it of yourself
either. We show them for the beauty of chess, and because their deep ideas may inspire you to
find the right move when you face a difficult decision in your own games. They are listed in the
index under \emph{Study-like positions}.

\section*{How the book is organised}
\xref{Chapter}{ch:meet} shows which endgames you will meet most often. \xref{Chapter}{ch:go-wrong}
shows which ones go wrong most often once they are on the board. \xref{Chapter}{ch:where-to-start}
puts the two together and chooses the endgames worth your time. From there on, each chapter takes
one of them, the simplest first: king and pawn endings, then endgames with one piece on each side,
and only then those with two pieces, however many points they cost. Within each of these groups,
the endgame that costs the most points comes first. You learn to walk before you learn to fly.
The bigger endgames so often turn into the smaller ones, and what you learn in a
simple endgame, such as how to use the king, is what you need in all the others. The appendix
explains how the numbers were made.

Enjoy the positions, and good luck in your next tournament!
